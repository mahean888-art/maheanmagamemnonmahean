Bioluminescence engineering

home

Bioluminescence is one of the few cases where a biological system does something we normally build machines for: it makes light, at ambient temperature, out of ordinary metabolism. For most of the history of the field that was a curiosity. Since about 2018 it has become an engineering problem — you can now move a complete light-producing pathway into an organism that never had one and have it glow continuously, without feeding it anything.

Some things I find striking

  • Bioluminescence has evolved independently at least 94 separate times. Almost nothing else in biology gets reinvented that often, which suggests the path to it is unusually short.
  • It is overwhelmingly a marine phenomenon. A large fraction of the animals in the deep ocean can produce light, and most of them do it with the same substrate — coelenterazine — which many of them cannot synthesize and instead acquire through diet.
  • Firefly luciferase converts chemical energy to photons at a quantum yield around 40%, which is remarkable for a room-temperature reaction in water.
  • The Hawaiian bobtail squid runs a light organ populated by Vibrio fischeri, which it acquires from seawater after hatching and vents most of every morning. The squid uses the light for counter-illumination — erasing its own silhouette against moonlight.
  • During WWII, Japanese soldiers reportedly carried dried Vargula hilgendorfii ostracods and wetted them in the palm for a dim reading light that gave off no heat and could not be seen from a distance.

The engineering shift

The bacterial lux operon (luxCDABE) has been portable for decades — it encodes both the luciferase and the enzymes that regenerate its substrate, so a bacterium carrying it glows autonomously. It never worked well in eukaryotes.

The unlock was the fungal pathway, characterized in 2018: four genes built on caffeic acid, a metabolite plants already make in quantity. Transferred into tobacco, they produced plants that glowed continuously across their whole life cycle without any added substrate. That is the first self-sustaining light pathway that composes cleanly with ordinary plant metabolism, and it is why a bioluminescent petunia could be sold to consumers in the US starting in 2024.

Separately, luciferases have been engineered hard as reporters rather than as light sources. NanoLuc, derived from a deep-sea shrimp enzyme, is about 19 kDa and vastly brighter than the firefly enzyme per molecule — bright enough that single-molecule and whole-animal imaging become routine.

Open questions

  • How far is the brightness ceiling? Engineered plants are clearly visible in a dark room but are still orders of magnitude away from useful illumination. Is the limit the flux through the substrate pathway, the enzyme turnover, or the plant's tolerance for diverting carbon?
  • Should the goal even be illumination? Per photon, an LED wins decisively and will keep winning. The comparative advantage of a living light is that it is coupled to state — it can report on what the organism is sensing. A plant that glows where its roots hit a contaminant is not competing with a lamp.
  • What is the actual cost to the organism? Published fitness costs in engineered plants seem low, which is surprising for a pathway running continuously. If it really is nearly free, that is the most interesting number in the field.
  • Why did the fungal pathway take so long to find, given how many independent origins exist? How many more complete, portable pathways are sitting uncharacterized in the other ~90?
  • Can the emission spectrum be tuned freely, or does color engineering always trade against brightness? Color-shifted variants exist, but the tradeoff curve is not well mapped.
